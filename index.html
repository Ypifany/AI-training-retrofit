AI Retrofit Playbook

How to add AI to a lesson we already teach: slowly, responsibly, and at little to no cost. The process below works for any lesson in any program. The worked example uses a real Cyber's Future deck, Session 2: Ethics and Core IT and Security Principles.

The ground rules

Every retrofit follows these five rules. They are what makes the rollout slow and responsible instead of fast and sloppy.

One touchpoint per lessonAI enters one existing activity. It never becomes the lesson.
AI after the first attemptThe student decodes, runs, or writes first. AI is the second opinion.
Verification is gradedThe student proves the AI right or wrong with the tool in front of him.
Disclosure, no penaltyOne line on the submission saying how AI helped. Never affects the grade.
Little to no costThe Gem we already deploy, plus free and open-source tools.

The process, for any lesson

Six moves. The lesson's learning objectives stay. One new objective is added. Assessment does not change.

1
Pick one existing activity.

Choose the activity where a working professional would already be using AI. Do not write a new activity.

2
Choose the touchpoint type.

Before (AI as a coach to get unstuck), During (AI interprets a tool's output), or After (AI reviews the student's finished work). One per lesson.

3
Keep the first attempt human.

The AI step always comes after the student's own attempt. If AI would erase the struggle the activity exists for, move the touchpoint later.

4
Make verification the submission.

Whatever the AI said, the student's deliverable is proof it was right or wrong. This maps to the Process and Independence bucket on the FK Assessment, so grading does not change.

5
Add one learning objective.

Write it in standard form, for example: "Students will verify AI-generated output using an independent tool." The lesson's existing objectives stay as they are.

6
Add the disclosure line.

The submission includes one sentence on how AI helped. Disclosure is the habit being built; it never affects the grade.

Beyond one program: the same process, three swaps

The six moves do not change across contexts. Three parameters do. Swap these and the playbook retrofits a CompTIA course, compliance training, or vendor training the same way it retrofits a STEM lesson.

The AI assistant

A youth program uses its approved coach tool. A certification course uses the student's chat assistant. A company uses whatever its policy has approved. In corporate settings this swap is a compliance decision, not a preference.

The assessment target

Verification maps to whatever instrument already grades the course: skill rubrics in a STEM program, exam objectives in a CompTIA course, policy and scenario checks in compliance training. No new assessment system, ever.

What rule 3 protects

Student privacy in a youth program. Company and client data in corporate training, where pasting the wrong thing into an AI tool can itself be a reportable incident. In those versions, this rule moves from ground rule to lesson content.

One real adaptation: training with no hands-on project (much compliance training) shifts the verification target from "check the AI against your tool" to "check the AI against the policy." Same move, different reference document.

When the AI acts: two added rules for agents

Generative AI answers questions. An agent takes actions: it runs the scan, files the ticket, executes the steps. The six moves still apply, and agent touchpoints add two rules.

Scope before action

The agent gets written boundaries before it acts: what it may touch, what it may never touch. Security students already learn this as Rules of Engagement. An agent gets the same contract a penetration tester does.

Review the actions, not just the answer

With generative AI, the learner verifies the output. With an agent, the learner reviews the action log: every step it took, and whether each one was allowed and correct. A human checkpoint sits before any action that changes something.

Sequence for introducing agents: first generative touchpoints (this playbook), then watching an agent work and auditing its log, then directing an agent inside boundaries the learner wrote. Advanced cybersecurity students reach step three inside the existing curriculum, where the defensive automation project already requires a human-in-the-loop checkpoint.

Evaluate along the way

No new instruments. All four measures come from systems already running.

Process & Independence

On the FK Assessment Form: does this bucket hold or drop on the AI-touched project compared to the student's previous project? A drop means the AI did the thinking.

Disclosure rate

Share of submissions that include the one-line AI disclosure. This is the honesty habit, measured.

The defense question

The instructor asks each student to explain one AI-assisted part without the AI. Pass rate is the independence metric.

Errors caught

Count of times a student correctly overruled the AI. This is judgment, measured, and it is the number that proves the approach works.
