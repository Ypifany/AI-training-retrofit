AN YPIFANY PROCESS

AI Retrofit Playbook

Keep the course you already teach. Add one AI step, and build the thing a tool cannot give you: judgment you can stand behind.

One useful AI step, added after an activity students already do. Nothing gets replaced. It costs little or nothing, and it is shown below on a real deck and on real certification questions.

THE ONE IDEA

Own what you put your name on.

Every rule here comes from one place. A student is answerable for what he shares, and for what an AI does in his name. So he works first, checks the AI, and decides what he will stand behind. The moment he approves what the AI produced, it is his work, whether or not he understood how the AI got there. It is also how the program keeps AI use ethical, safe, and secure: permission before action, privacy by default, and a person answerable at every step.

The six rules

Six moves, in order, starting with the lesson you already have. The lesson's objectives stay, one new objective is added, and the course is never replaced. The rules keep the impact high without adding much cost.

1
Pick one existing activity.

Choose the activity where a working professional would already be using AI. Do not write a new activity.

2
Place the AI step in the workflow.

Decide two things. When it enters: before the work to get unstuck, during the work on a tool's output, or after the work to review what the student did. And how far it reaches: inside what the student can already do, or out at the edge of it as a stretch. One placement per lesson.

3
Keep the first attempt human.

The AI step always comes after the student's own attempt. If AI would erase the struggle the activity exists for, move the touchpoint later.

4
Make the check the submission.

What the student turns in is his check of the AI, not the AI's answer: where it is right, where it is wrong, and what he will stand behind. This maps onto any existing course assessment. If you add a new criterion, like "caught the AI's mistakes," tell students up front. How far the AI reaches is the second part of rule 2, and when it reaches to the edge, the check adds one more line, shown in The edge below.

5
Add one learning objective.

Write it in standard form, for example: "Students will verify AI-generated output using an independent tool." The lesson's existing objectives stay as they are.

6
Add the disclosure line.

The submission includes one sentence on how AI helped. Disclosure is the habit being built.

The edge: how far the AI reaches

This is the second part of rule 2, how far the AI reaches. Picture a tether between the student and the work. At the edge the tether is still taut, so he can pull the work back and check it. Past the edge it goes slack, and he is trusting what he cannot see. The edge is where the real growth happens, as long as it is handled right.

·
Inside his reach.

The AI works on ground the student already knows, so he can check everything it hands back against what he can already do. Most steps live here, and they are safe.

·
At the edge, a stretch.

The AI takes him further than he could go alone, but not so far that he is lost. He can still reel the work back, test it against the real thing in front of him, and explain it before he signs it. Every time he does, his reach moves out and the next stretch starts further along.

·
Past the edge, blind trust.

The AI hands him something that works and that he cannot explain or check. It looks right, he signs it, and he could not catch it if it were wrong. This is the place to keep students out of, and it is what the stretch is built to prevent.

THE STRETCH SUBMISSION

When a step reaches to the edge, the student turns in three things: the work, one line on how he knows it did what he asked, and the one thing he had to look up to be sure. If he cannot write that line, the step went past the edge, and that is the signal for the instructor.

When the AI acts: two added rules for agents

Same one idea, higher stakes. With generative AI you are answerable for what you share. An agent acts: it takes steps on its own, so now you are answerable for what it does in your name. That is why an agent gets two more rules. An agent is the far edge of that reach, the biggest stretch there is, and these two rules are how the tether stays taut even there. Don't read about them, try them. Picture your home office. You gave an agent this job: "Sort this week's receipts and draft a reply to my landlord about the rent." Now run it, and read what it does.

RULE 6 · SCOPE BEFORE ACTION

Before the agent starts, the learner writes its boundaries:

  • May look at: your inbox and Receipts folder only
  • May sort receipts and draft a reply
  • May not open saved files outside the inbox
  • May not send anything until you say yes

This is the same contract a security student writes before a test, called Rules of Engagement. You are writing one for a chore at home: here is what you may touch, and nothing goes out without me.

Rule 7 · Human checkpoint. The agent cannot send without you. You have its draft above and its four steps in the log. Do you let the reply go out?

Rollout order: first generative touchpoints (this playbook), then auditing an agent's log the way you just did, then directing an agent inside boundaries the learner wrote. Advanced cybersecurity students reach that third step inside the existing curriculum, where the defensive automation project already requires a human-in-the-loop checkpoint.

ABOUT REAL AGENT TOOLS

Agents are real. Student access is not a given. Check first.

Agents are here. Gemini, for example, now has a Task mode that does jobs on its own across a person's Google apps and can run on a schedule. But the access is gated. As of its current documentation, Task needs an adult account (18 or older), a paid Gemini plan, and it excludes work and school accounts. For a grades 6 to 12 program, that means most students cannot turn it on, and no one should try it on a school account. Provider rules like these change often, so verify them before you rely on any agent feature.

RULE 6 · SET THE LIMITS FIRSTBefore an agent runs, you name what it may touch and who approves a change. You own those limits; the AI does not set them, and a prompt that says "do not share" is not the same as switching sharing off.
RULE 7 · REVIEW WHAT IT DIDRead the action log, check the real draft and recipient, and sign off before anything leaves the room. You are answerable for what it did in your name. A summary of what it says it did is not the record.

The core of this playbook needs none of that. Students practice with ordinary AI and the prepared simulation above. No student needs to buy an agent plan or connect a real inbox to learn this.

TRY IT Two real exam questions, and the move behind each

Ask an AI either question below and it answers well. It will even flag the traps itself. That is the point, and the problem. The exam and the AI both stop at knowing the answer. The job starts one step later, at the move only the analyst can make: go to the real system, settle which answer is true there, and sign your name to it. Here are two objectives, and the move behind each.

CySA+ · Objective 2.2 Interpreting vulnerability assessment results

On the exam: a static scan flags a possible SQL injection, a dynamic scan reports nothing, but the dynamic scan's session expired right after login so it never tested the signed-in pages. Exam answer: D, keep the finding open and fix the coverage before retesting. Ask an AI and it gets this right. On the exam, AI wins.

ON THE JOB · WHERE AI ENTERS · YOUR NEXT MOVE

You are the analyst now. To triage the conflict fast, you ask an AI, and it gives a genuinely good answer:

THE REAL AI ANSWER

"A clean DAST scan does not mean the SAST finding is a false positive. The gap is common and needs manual triage. DAST only tests what it can reach, so it may miss a real bug because of coverage and crawling limits, authentication issues like a dropped session, complex execution paths, or a blind SQL injection."

The AI gave you the map, not the answer. What is your next move?

That is the move. The AI gave you the map: four reasons a scan can miss a real bug. It cannot tell you which one is true in your portal, because it cannot see your portal. So you go to the real evidence, the session log and the live endpoint, settle which cause is real, and decide. The AI reasons for free. The checking against your system, and the finding you sign, are the job.
CySA+ · Objective 3.3 Incident response and evidence integrity

On the exam: an endpoint clock ran seven minutes fast during an incident, the firewall clock was right, and the original logs are already hashed. Exam answer: A, normalize the times in a separate copy, document it, and keep the originals untouched. Ask an AI and it gets this right, and it will even build the timeline for you.

ON THE JOB · WHERE AI ENTERS · YOUR NEXT MOVE

You ask the AI to normalize the times and build the timeline. It does it right, on a working copy, and hands it back:

THE REAL AI ANSWER

"Because the endpoint clock was seven minutes fast, I subtracted seven minutes from its events in a working copy, kept the firewall times as they were, documented the offset, and left the hashed originals untouched. Timeline attached."

The method is right. Before you put your name on it, what is your next move?

That is the move. The AI did it right, and you still cannot sign what you did not check. The AI cannot see whether your originals are intact, or whether the copy in front of you is the one going in the report. So you open the evidence, confirm it against the originals, and put your name on it. Verifying is not doubt about the AI. It is ownership, and the name on the report is yours, not the tool's.

Two objectives, one pattern. The AI hands over a strong, correct answer in seconds, and it still cannot see your system or sign your name. The move against the real evidence, and the finding you own, is the job. That is what the exam assumes and the employer pays for, and it is the one thing AI does not touch.

Evaluate it with the Ypifany Evaluation Model

A model of my own, ten years in the making. It does not grade from the outside. It treats evaluation as noticing and making meaning of what emerges in a system, so the student's own work is the evidence and the instructor reads it from inside the room, not from above it.

SELF-DIRECTED EVALUATION

The design and the evaluation run on one idea.

"Keep the first attempt human" and "make the check the submission" are self-directed evaluation: the student notices his own thinking and makes it visible. The three indicators below are what the instructor notices and mirrors back, reported as "here is what I saw from where I stand," not "here is what is true."

THREE NATURAL INDICATORS · ILLUSTRATIVE NUMBERS, NOT REAL DATA
Appropriate reliance
0 of 14 calibrated

Across AI-assisted work, did the student keep the AI when it was right and overrule it when it was wrong? Both sides count: keeping a wrong answer is over-reliance, throwing out a right one is under-reliance. This is the documented measure of AI judgment, not just catching mistakes.

Unaided transfer
0 of 14 held or rose

On the program's skills assessment, can the student still do or explain the task with the AI closed? This is learning that transfers to the job, which is the level training is finally judged on, not help in the moment.

Reach extended
0 of 14 moved up a level

Students who can now do unaided what used to need a stretch, moving toward the next SFIA proficiency level. This is reach moving out, which is the point of the edge.
