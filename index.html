AN YPIFANY PROCESS

AI Retrofit Playbook

Keep the course you already teach. Add one AI step, and build the thing a tool cannot give you: judgment you can stand behind.

One useful AI step, added after an activity students already do. Nothing gets replaced. It costs little or nothing, and it is shown below on a real deck and on real certification questions.

THE ONE IDEA

Own what you put your name on.

Every rule here comes from one place. A student is answerable for what he shares, and for what an AI does in his name. So he works first, checks the AI, and decides what he will stand behind. It is also how the program keeps AI use ethical, safe, and secure: permission before action, privacy by default, and a person answerable at every step.

The six rules

Six moves, in order, starting with the lesson you already have. The lesson's objectives stay, one new objective is added, and the course is never replaced. The rules keep the impact high without adding much cost.

1
Pick one existing activity.

Choose the activity where a working professional would already be using AI. Do not write a new activity.

2
Choose the touchpoint type.

Before (AI as a coach to get unstuck), During (AI reads a tool's output, or AI is the tool the student works with), or After (AI reviews the student's finished work). One per lesson.

3
Keep the first attempt human.

The AI step always comes after the student's own attempt. If AI would erase the struggle the activity exists for, move the touchpoint later.

4
Make the check the submission.

The student's deliverable is his check of what the AI said: where it holds, where it fails, and what he will stand behind. This maps onto whatever already grades the course, such as the Process and Independence bucket on the FK Assessment. If you add a new criterion, name it up front.

5
Add one learning objective.

Write it in standard form, for example: "Students will verify AI-generated output using an independent tool." The lesson's existing objectives stay as they are.

6
Add the disclosure line.

The submission includes one sentence on how AI helped. Disclosure is the habit being built.

When the AI acts: two added rules for agents

Same one idea, higher stakes. With generative AI you are answerable for what you share. An agent acts: it takes steps on its own, so now you are answerable for what it does in your name. That is why an agent gets two more rules. Don't read about them, try them. Picture your home office. You gave an agent this job: "Sort this week's receipts and draft a reply to my landlord about the rent." Now run it, and read what it does.

RULE 6 · SCOPE BEFORE ACTION

Before the agent starts, the learner writes its boundaries:

  • May look at: your inbox and Receipts folder only
  • May sort receipts and draft a reply
  • May not open saved files outside the inbox
  • May not send anything until you say yes

This is the same contract a security student writes before a test, called Rules of Engagement. You are writing one for a chore at home: here is what you may touch, and nothing goes out without me.

Rollout order: first generative touchpoints (this playbook), then auditing an agent's log the way you just did, then directing an agent inside boundaries the learner wrote. Advanced cybersecurity students reach that third step inside the existing curriculum, where the defensive automation project already requires a human-in-the-loop checkpoint.

ABOUT REAL AGENT TOOLS

Agents are real. Student access is not a given. Check first.

Agents are here. Gemini, for example, now has a Task mode that does jobs on its own across a person's Google apps and can run on a schedule. But the access is gated. As of its current documentation, Task needs an adult account (18 or older), a paid Gemini plan, and it excludes work and school accounts. For a grades 6 to 12 program, that means most students cannot turn it on, and no one should try it on a school account. Provider rules like these change often, so verify them before you rely on any agent feature.

RULE 6 · SET THE LIMITS FIRSTBefore an agent runs, you name what it may touch and who approves a change. You own those limits; the AI does not set them, and a prompt that says "do not share" is not the same as switching sharing off.
RULE 7 · REVIEW WHAT IT DIDRead the action log, check the real draft and recipient, and sign off before anything leaves the room. You are answerable for what it did in your name. A summary of what it says it did is not the record.

The core of this playbook needs none of that. Students practice with ordinary AI and the prepared simulation above. No student needs to buy an agent plan or connect a real inbox to learn this.

The same process works in other training

The six moves and the ground rules never change, and the existing course is never replaced. Moving this process to a different kind of training changes only three things: which AI the learner uses, what the learner checks the AI against, and whose private information gets protected. For certification training: the job test the exam skips

A certificate proves you can pick the right answer on a test. The job asks something harder: can you keep your judgment when a confident tool hands you a wrong one? No exam item covers that, and it is exactly what the retrofit adds. Here are real exam-style questions with one AI step added. Adding AI will not help anyone pass a proctored exam, and we do not pretend it will. It builds the judgment the exam assumes and the employer actually pays for.

CySA+ · Objective 1.4 Threat intelligence and threat hunting

The exam item, unchangedThree vendor reports link a file hash to a ransomware group. All three copied the attribution from the same anonymous forum post. The file is confirmed malicious, but nothing identifies the operator. The most defensible assessment: maliciousness is supported, but attribution stays uncertain because the reports are not independent.

The AI step, newAsk any AI "who is behind this hash?" It will name the group with confidence, blending the same recycled sources. The student makes the same call on the AI's answer that the exam wants on the vendor reports: supported where there is independent evidence, uncertain where there is not.

What it buildsThe habit of separating "confident" from "corroborated." The AI commits the exact error the objective tests, so the student practices the real skill on a live example.

CySA+ · Objective 3.3 Incident response and evidence integrity

The exam item, unchangedAn analyst rebuilds an incident from endpoint and firewall logs. The endpoint clock ran seven minutes fast; the originals are already collected and hashed. The correct approach: normalize timestamps in a separate analysis copy, document the adjustment, and keep the originals untouched.

The AI step, newAsk an AI to "build the timeline from these logs." It will happily adjust timestamps, and will sometimes imply editing the originals. The student checks the AI's approach against chain of custody: would this survive in a report someone has to defend? He keeps what preserves the evidence and rejects what breaks it.

What it buildsThe instinct to protect the original record even when a tool offers a faster shortcut. The exam tests the rule; the job tests whether you hold the line under a confident suggestion.

CGRC / RMF Control assessment and documentation

The exam item, unchangedThe exam tests whether you know what a control requires and how the RMF steps fit together. It does not test how the control narrative gets written.

The AI step, newOn the job, people draft control narratives, SSP language, and POA&M entries with AI. It produces fluent text that often does not match the real system. The learner drafts with AI, then checks each claim against the actual configuration and the control's intent, and marks what is true, what is boilerplate, and what is missing.

What it buildsThe assessor's core skill: telling "sounds compliant" from "is compliant." The exam never tests it. The job is almost nothing but it.

The pattern is the same every time. Take the question you already study. Add the AI where a working professional would reach for it. Then make the student answerable for the call. The certificate gets the interview; this gets the job.

Evaluate along the way

Three measures, each one documented in training and human-AI research, not invented here. Two come straight from the FK Assessment the program already runs; the first is read off the one-line AI check each lesson already asks for. Here is what one class of 14 could look like after one cycle.

SAMPLE VIEW · ILLUSTRATIVE NUMBERS, NOT REAL DATA
Appropriate reliance
0 of 14 calibrated

Across AI-assisted work, did the student keep the AI when it was right and overrule it when it was wrong? Both sides count: keeping a wrong answer is over-reliance, throwing out a right one is under-reliance. This is the documented measure of AI judgment, not just catching mistakes.

Unaided transfer
0 of 14 held or rose

On the FK Assessment, can the student still do or explain the task with the AI closed? This is learning that transfers to the job, which is the level training is finally judged on, not help in the moment.

Proficiency movement
0 of 14 moved up a level

Students who moved toward the next SFIA proficiency level this cycle. The workforce outcome families and funders care about.

WHAT TO DO NEXT

Pick one lesson you already teach. Add a single AI step after an activity students already do, write one new learning objective, and run it once. That is the whole first step. To go deeper, open the click-through page on a screen with your instructors and walk one lesson together.
