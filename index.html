AN YPIFANY PROCESS

AI Retrofit Playbook

How to add AI to a course you already teach: slowly, responsibly, and at little to no cost. Nothing gets replaced. The existing slides and activities stay, and one AI step is added after them as an extension of the same task. The process works for any lesson in any training program, and it is shown below on a real deck.

The ground rules

Every retrofit follows these five rules. They are what makes the rollout slow and responsible instead of fast and sloppy.

One touchpoint per lessonAI enters one existing activity. It never becomes the lesson.
AI after the first attemptThe student decodes, runs, or writes first. AI is the second opinion.
Verification is gradedThe student proves the AI right or wrong with the tool in front of him.
Disclosure, no penaltyOne line on the submission saying how AI helped. Never affects the grade.
Little to no costFree tiers, open tools, or the AI the program already pays for.

The process, for any lesson

Six moves. The lesson's learning objectives stay. One new objective is added. Assessment does not change.

1
Pick one existing activity.

Choose the activity where a working professional would already be using AI. Do not write a new activity.

2
Choose the touchpoint type.

Before (AI as a coach to get unstuck), During (AI interprets a tool's output), or After (AI reviews the student's finished work). One per lesson.

3
Keep the first attempt human.

The AI step always comes after the student's own attempt. If AI would erase the struggle the activity exists for, move the touchpoint later.

4
Make verification the submission.

Whatever the AI said, the student's deliverable is proof it was right or wrong. This maps onto whatever already grades the course — in the worked example, the Process and Independence bucket on the FK Assessment — so grading does not change.

5
Add one learning objective.

Write it in standard form, for example: "Students will verify AI-generated output using an independent tool." The lesson's existing objectives stay as they are.

6
Add the disclosure line.

The submission includes one sentence on how AI helped. Disclosure is the habit being built; it never affects the grade.

The same process works in other training

The six moves and the ground rules never change, and the existing course is never replaced. Moving this process to a different kind of training changes only three things: which AI the learner uses, what the learner checks the AI against, and whose private information gets protected. When the AI acts: two added rules for agents

Generative AI answers questions. An agent does work on its own: you give it a job, it takes the steps, and you see what it did afterward. That adds two rules. Don't read about them — try them. You gave an agent this job: "Check the practice lab's computers for weak spots and write me a report." Now run it, and read what it does.

RULE 6 · SCOPE BEFORE ACTION

Before the agent starts, the learner writes its boundaries:

  • May look at: the practice lab only
  • May write up what it finds
  • May not touch anything outside the lab
  • May not send anything until a human says yes

Security students already know this contract: Rules of Engagement. An agent signs the same one a penetration tester does.

Every step the agent takes will show up here, in order. This is its action log.
THE REPORT IT WANTS TO SEND

"Practice lab check complete. Found 3 weak spots: two old passwords and one unlocked shared folder. Recommend fixing before next session."

Rule 7 · Human checkpoint. The agent cannot send without you. You have its report above and its four steps in the log. Do you let the report go out?

Rollout order: first generative touchpoints (this playbook), then auditing an agent's log the way you just did, then directing an agent inside boundaries the learner wrote. Advanced cybersecurity students reach that third step inside the existing curriculum, where the defensive automation project already requires a human-in-the-loop checkpoint.

THIS IS NOT HYPOTHETICAL

Your students already have an agent: Gemini's Task mode

The lab agent above runs on a walled-off Kali machine. But Gemini now has an agent anyone can turn on, and it lives in Google's cloud and acts inside your own Google apps. Its Task mode does jobs on its own: it reads and sorts your Gmail, changes your calendar, builds files, and even opens websites to shop or book things. It can run on a schedule, like every morning. That is a real agent, and the two rules fit it word for word. Google built both of them right into the controls.

RULE 6 · SCOPE BEFORE ACTIONBefore it runs, you pick what it can reach. Google's own warning: never put passwords or private details into the task. That is scope, in their words.
RULE 7 · REVIEW THE ACTIONSTask shows you every step it took and every file it opened, and lets you stop it. It also asks before it sends, buys, or changes anything. That is the human checkpoint, built in.

One honest limit: Task needs a paid Gemini plan and a personal Google account, so not every student will have it. But the moment one does turn it on, he is supervising an agent, and this playbook is how he does it safely.
